Deciding What to Build in LineLeap Social

Four rounds of research to find a direction for LineLeap's social feature, and the evidence that there wasn't one worth building.

User research Synthesis Personas Concept testing
Company
LineLeap
Role
UX Researcher
Timeline
May 2023 – June 2024
Partners
Product, design, engineering, data
The brief
Get the app used across the whole night, not just for cover at the start. Specifically: build posting habits that don't depend on paying people to post.
What I found
Users were producing content and almost nobody was consuming it. Rewards drove 88.8% of posting, and the friends graph barely existed.
What changed
I recommended against building further into social. People already have social apps, a night out is the worst moment to ask for attention, and bars have bad reception.

01

The challenge

LineLeap was known for one thing: skipping the line at a bar. People opened it once, at the start of the night, bought cover, and put their phone away. The goal was to extend that into the rest of the evening, so the app was part of the whole night instead of its first ten minutes, and LineLeap's reach grew with it.

Social features were the proposed route there. The problem statement I started from:

How can LineLeap increase engagement through social features to attain its business OKRs?

People were already posting, but almost all of it was for the daily rewards. The real question was whether that could become a natural habit: would anyone keep posting if we stopped paying them?

02

My role

UX Researcher. I ran the interview and survey rounds, did the synthesis, built the personas and reports, and ran the workshop that turned findings into feature concepts.

03

How I approached it

4 rounds, pre- and post-launch
55 user interviews
400+ survey responses

Totals across all four rounds. The round below was 184 respondents and 7 interviews.

Segmented by behavior, not self-report

Much of the posting volume was blank screens uploaded to claim the daily reward. Surveying "social users" as one group would have averaged that away, so I split the sample by what people had actually posted: blank screens, St. Patrick's Day promo posts, and real photos outside any promo.

The blank-screen group was entirely reward-driven. All of them posted for the daily reward, five of seven wouldn't post without it, and they were the least comfortable posting publicly. So the blank screens weren't users gaming the system. They were users who wanted the reward and didn't want to be seen.

What this research couldn't tell us

  • We recruited from people who had used the feature and paid them in LineLeap credit, which over-represents the reward motivation the study was measuring.
  • Every interview participant this round was a woman.
  • Many questions were hypothetical, and people are bad at predicting themselves. Part of why I segmented on behavior.

04

What we learned

Users are producing content. Almost nobody is consuming it. LineLeap had successfully paid people to post, and had not built a feed anyone went out of their way to read. Users called social the "cherry on top" of the app, not the reason they opened it.

We ask for attention at the worst moment of the night

Going out is chaotic. People are drinking, talking to the friends they came with, often on bad reception, and most only open social while at the bar. Rewards bought production. Consumption competes with the entire night.

So stop asking users to work harder at a feed: LineLeap can tell someone where their friends are from its own data instead of waiting for those friends to post it.

Rewards drive production, and nobody can explain them

  • 88.8% post for rewards. Fun is second at 63.7%, "my friends do it" third at 44.7%.
  • About half couldn't say how they were earning points.
  • Half didn't know they auto-earn after redeeming a pass.
  • Rewards work per venue, which is most of why they read as unattainable.

A comprehension problem before a generosity problem, which mattered because the instinct in the room was to add more rewards.

Consumption breaks at friend-adding

Users add friends in the first few minutes after download or never. Contact books don't match who they want to follow. Pending requests go unnoticed.

"I don't think I have friends. When I check there are people who come up, it's from contacts, it's too random of people for me to want to add them."

So the friends feed is empty and explore is too random. But the obvious fix was the wrong one: only 18% would stop posting if friends didn't look, and 57% disagreed. Audience size barely matters. Audience identity does.

"I don't have their phone number, but I might still hang out with them at night and want to see their pictures."

Low-stakes discovery already existed and nobody had noticed: tapping a post to see who gave it gems, to "see whose name I recognize," without the commitment of a follow request.

There is no single answer on audience

Nobody was sure what "public" meant. Described as the city or the internet, people hesitated. Described as the other people at the bar, the hesitation dropped. Safety was part of it, and school-email verification came up unprompted. That is the other half of the blank-screen finding.

What works: the status, and the prompt

The status is "the heart of the post." The pre-written options are funny and relatable, picking one is a low-stakes way to show what you're up to, and they're cheap to retune to pop culture and college sports.

83% enjoy posting in real time, and participants kept reaching for BeReal as the comparison. Enthusiasm isn't uniform, but the objections are specific and fixable.

Same rule for competitions: low stakes beats a big prize. The Halloween contest worked because it took a photo people were already taking.

The venue is loyalty LineLeap already has

Students are devoted to their bar, follow it on Instagram for information that is often stale, and value venue merch well above generic rewards. Venue involvement made them measurably more willing to enter a competition. Two directions came out of it: a person posting from the bar rather than a brand account, and venue-hosted competitions with venue-provided prizes.

None of it generalizes evenly

A smaller market, defined by how established LineLeap is there rather than by school size, behaves differently from a mature one. Low presence means an empty feed, and empty feeds don't recruit posters. Market context got its own section in the report so the business could work campus by campus.

05

How the synthesis worked

  1. Who, then why. Lead with personas so findings belong to a person, not to "users." That included the heavy users: why do the people who use it constantly love it?
  2. Findings per persona, per topic. Personality type drove most of the variance, and keeping findings split by persona stopped it averaging into a useless middle.
  3. Group into themes, then map each persona back onto each theme. The layer I added after a first pass stopped at the three groups. What they have in common is the only thing one feature can serve.
  4. State a thesis the evidence has to support. Ours: what people post is unique, constantly changing, and worth saving. Every finding linked back to it, so a concept that didn't fit had to justify itself.
  5. Concept cards. A scrappy long list, grouped by theme, narrowed to seven or eight, each traceable to a theme and a user need.

06

Artifacts, and taking it to the business

  • A survey report, and an interview report broken down by user, so a PM could read one persona end to end instead of scanning a flat list of quotes.
  • Personas divided by user need, each need mapped to the business OKR it served. That mapping made the research legible to stakeholders accountable to OKRs, and doubled as the spec for what the data team needed to instrument.
  • Concept cards as the handoff object, so product and design chose between specific, evidence-backed options.
  • A usability list kept separate from the strategic work, so small fixes weren't stuck behind big decisions: disliked auto flash, posts defaulting to "out and about" instead of the bar the user was standing in, no way to save a photo you'd posted.

A workshop, not a presentation

I brought the synthesis to business stakeholders unfinished on purpose and had them group the findings with me, then generate features against the areas we identified together. Stakeholders who grouped the findings themselves didn't need selling on the conclusions afterward.

Reporting a non-result

We had shipped two post flows and wanted to know which to keep. The survey came back 45% to 55%, which is not a preference. The report said so and moved the decision to other grounds.

07

What I concluded

LineLeap social didn't fit, and I recommended against building further into it. That wasn't a design problem, and a better feed would not have fixed it. Four things worked against it at once:

  • People already have social apps. Nothing LineLeap could build was going to displace iMessage, Instagram or BeReal, and it was competing with them at the hour they get used most.
  • The moment is wrong. A night out is chaotic. People are drinking, talking to the friends they came with, and not looking for another app to open.
  • The infrastructure is against it. Bars have bad reception. A feed that won't load is not a feed.
  • Nothing pulled. Rewards pushed production, and what they produced was never distinctive enough to pull anyone into consuming it. Take the rewards away and the posting goes too.

The goal was more of the night. The research found that the night is precisely when people have the least attention to give, and that the habit the business wanted to build had nothing underneath it but the rewards paying for it.

What held up anyway

A negative result on the big question still left decisions the research could settle:

  • Rewards were treated as a comprehension problem rather than a generosity one, since users couldn't explain the system driving 88.8% of their posting.
  • Friend-adding moved from a sign-up step to an ongoing one, because the graph is built in the first five minutes or never.
  • Direct messaging was dropped outright.
  • The venue relationship was the one place real loyalty already existed, and it didn't need manufacturing.
  • Market context became a standing section of the reporting, so findings from a mature campus stopped being applied to a new one.

The research was commissioned to find a direction. The most useful thing it did was establish there wasn't one worth the spend, before more of it went out the door.
