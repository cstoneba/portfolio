Deciding What to Build in LineLeap Social

LineLeap had business goals riding on a social feature and a long list of candidate directions. Four rounds of research to work out which ones users would actually engage with, and to say plainly when the answer was that they wouldn't.

01

The challenge

LineLeap was known for one thing: skipping the line at a bar. The company wanted to expand into a social platform, and it had business OKRs riding on engagement with it. The question was never whether to build social features. It was which ones.

The problem statement I started from:

How can LineLeap increase engagement through social features to attain its business OKRs?

That is a business question, and it isn't testable as written. By the round documented here it had sharpened into one I could actually run research against: how can LineLeap better facilitate interactions between users and their friends, and amplify the fun they are already having, in order to build posting habits that are not dependent on rewards.

The last clause is the whole problem. People were posting. They were posting because they were paid to.

02

My role

UX Researcher. I planned and ran the interview and survey rounds, did the synthesis, built the personas and the reports, and ran the workshop that turned findings into feature concepts for product and design to take forward.

03

How I approached it

I wasn't starting cold. On earlier projects I had already interviewed and surveyed LineLeap social users and built personas for the main groups. What we didn't have was direction. Attitudes tell you what is happening now, not which way to build.

4 research rounds, pre- and post-launch
55 user interviews
400+ survey responses

Those are the totals across all four rounds. The round this case study draws on was 184 survey respondents and 7 interviews.

Segmenting by behavior instead of self-report

A lot of the posting volume was people uploading blank or black screens to claim the daily reward. Surveying "social users" as one group would have averaged that away, so I split the sample by what people had actually posted: blank screens (Group A), posts during the St. Patrick's Day promo (Group B), and real photos outside any promo (Group C).

That split did most of the analytical work, because it compares motivation across observed behavior instead of asking people to explain their own habits. Group A came back almost entirely reward-driven: every one of them posted for the daily reward, and five of seven agreed they wouldn't post without it. They were also the group least comfortable posting to a public feed. Those two facts together reframed what the blank screens were. Not users gaming the system, but users who wanted the reward and didn't want to be seen.

Four interview topics, each a candidate direction

  • The feed itself. How people use it, who they share with, what actually motivates a post.
  • Venue engagement. Students are intensely loyal to their bar. Could LineLeap carry any of that, and how do people engage with the venue today?
  • Groups and peer-to-peer. We had floated direct messaging before and heard that people already use iMessage while out, so this round went deeper into groups: what size, who runs them, what they would be for.
  • Competitions. Who people would compete against, over what, for what prize, and how publicly.

What this research could not tell us

I put the limitations in the report itself rather than in an appendix, because one of them bears directly on the headline finding.

  • Recruiting bias. We recruited from people who had used the feature and paid them in LineLeap credit. That sample is very likely to over-represent people motivated by rewards, which is exactly the variable the research was about.
  • Sample composition. Every interview participant in this round was a woman.
  • Hypotheticals. A lot of the questions asked people to predict their own behavior in a situation they weren't in, and people are bad at that. It is part of why I leaned on observed posting behavior for the segmentation instead of trusting self-report.

04

What we learned

The finding the whole round turned on: users are producing content, but almost nobody is consuming it. LineLeap had successfully paid people to post. What it had not done was make a feed anyone went out of their way to look at. Users described the social feature as the "cherry on top" of the app, a bonus rather than the reason they opened it, and the content being produced was not distinctive enough to pull anyone in.

We are asking for attention at the worst moment of the night

Going out is chaotic before, during and after. People are drinking, talking to the friends they came with, and frequently on bad reception. LineLeap had bought production with rewards, but consumption competes against the entire night, and most people said they only open the social feature while at the bar at all.

This reframed the brief. Rather than asking users to work harder at a feed, repackage what the app already knows: LineLeap can tell someone where their friends are from its own data instead of waiting for those friends to post it.

Rewards drive production, and almost nobody can explain them

  • 88.8% said they post for rewards. Fun came second at 63.7%, and "my friends do it" third at 44.7%.
  • About half couldn't say how they were earning points, and half didn't know they auto-earn after redeeming a pass.
  • Many had never heard of the 2x promo that was running at the time.

Rewards also work on a per-venue basis, which is most of why they read as difficult to attain. So the single biggest driver of engagement was a system users could not describe back to us. That made rewards a comprehension problem before a generosity problem, which mattered because the instinct in the room was to add more of them.

Consumption breaks at friend-adding

People said they wanted content from friends, and then rarely saw any, because the friends graph barely exists. Users generally add friends only in the first few minutes after downloading the app. Their contact books don't match the people they actually want to follow. Pending friend requests sit unnoticed.

"I don't think I have friends. When I check there are people who come up, it's from contacts, it's too random of people for me to want to add them."

Interview participant

So the friends feed is empty and the explore feed is too random to hold attention, and the user is left with neither. Which is also why the obvious fix was the wrong one: a user's decision to post is not actually gated on friends looking. Only 18% agreed they would stop posting if their friends didn't look at the feed, and 57% disagreed. Audience size barely matters. Audience identity does.

The clearest version of what people actually wanted was the peripheral friend:

"I don't have their phone number, but I might still hang out with them at night and want to see their pictures."

Interview participant, on who they want in their feed

Low-stakes discovery already existed in the product and nobody had noticed it was doing work: tapping a post to see who had given it gems was described as a way to "see whose name I recognize" without the commitment of a follow request.

Who people will share with varies more than the team assumed

There was no single answer to who the audience should be, which is a problem when the feed only offers one. Mapping each interviewee onto a private-to-open spectrum made that impossible to round off in the readout.

Nobody was sure what "public" even meant here. Described as the city or the internet, people hesitated. Described as the other people at the bar with them, the hesitation dropped. Part of the reservation was safety, and verification by school email came up unprompted as the fix. That is the other half of the Group A finding: blank screens were a privacy workaround for a feature whose audience had never been made legible.

What does work: the status, and the prompt

Two things in the product were genuinely loved, and both were cheap.

The first is the status. People called it "the heart of the post," found the pre-written options funny and relatable, and valued that picking one is a low-stakes way to show what you're up to without composing anything. Statuses are also low maintenance on LineLeap's side and can be retuned to pop culture, trends and college sports whenever they go stale.

The second is being prompted to take a photo in the moment. 83% agreed they enjoy posting in real time, and participants kept reaching for BeReal unprompted as the comparison. But enthusiasm for prompting is not uniform, and the objections were specific and fixable rather than general.

The same low-stakes rule governed competitions. People worried about losing and about spending money, so making clear there was no harm in entering mattered more than the prize. The Halloween costume contest worked because all it took was a photo people were already taking. Coordination while out is hard, which makes prompting a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

The venue is the loyalty LineLeap already has access to

Students are devoted to their bar. They follow it on Instagram for information that is frequently incomplete or out of date, they value venue merch and venue giveaways well above generic rewards, and venue involvement made them measurably more willing to enter a competition. Knowing the bar endorsed something was itself what made it worth doing. Two directions came out of that: personification of the venue, meaning a person posting conversationally rather than a brand account, and venue-hosted competitions with venue-provided prizes, where the prize mattered less than where it came from.

And none of it generalizes evenly

The finding I pushed hardest to get into the report was that a smaller market, defined by how established LineLeap is there rather than by the size of the school, behaves differently from a mature one. Low presence means an empty feed, and an empty feed does not recruit posters, so the same feature produces different results in each place. Giving market context its own section let the business apply different strategies campus by campus, and opened a path to growing through the social feature in markets where bars weren't signed yet.

05

How the synthesis worked

A pile of transcripts and a few headline findings is not a deliverable. The structure I used to get from one to the other, in order:

  1. Who, then why. Lead with the personas, so findings are read as belonging to a person rather than to "users." That included the heavy users. We deliberately asked why the people who use it constantly love it, instead of only studying the people who don't.
  2. Findings per persona, per topic. What each character said about the venue, groups, peer interaction and competitions. Personality type drove a lot of the variance, and keeping findings separated by persona is what stopped it averaging into a useless middle.
  3. Group findings into themes, then map each persona back onto each theme. The layer I added after a first pass that stopped at the three groups. The useful question wasn't what separates them but what all three have in common, because that common ground is the only thing a single feature can serve.
  4. State a thesis the evidence has to support. Ours was that what people post on LineLeap is unique, constantly changing, and worth saving. Every survey and interview finding got linked back to it, which made it a test rather than a slogan. A concept that didn't fit had to justify itself.
  5. Concept cards. A scrappy long list first, grouped by theme, narrowed to seven or eight polished concepts. Each one traceable to a theme, and through the theme to a user need.

06

Artifacts, and taking it to the business

The research only counted if the business could act on it, so every round ended in something other people could use rather than a readout nobody reopened.

  • A survey report, and a separate interview report broken down by user. Organizing the interview report per user meant a product manager could read one persona end to end instead of scanning a flat list of quotes.
  • Personas divided by user need, each need mapped to the business OKR it served. That mapping is what made the research legible to stakeholders accountable to OKRs rather than to the feed, and it doubled as the definition of what to instrument for the data team, who wanted OKR visibility and tracking.
  • Concept cards as the handoff object, so product and design were choosing between specific, evidence-backed options.
  • A usability list kept separate from the strategic work, so small fixes weren't held up behind big decisions: auto flash that people disliked, posts defaulting to a generic "out and about" instead of tagging the bar the user was standing in, no way to save a photo you'd posted, no refresh when a pass sold out.

Reporting a non-result

We had shipped two versions of the post flow and wanted to know which to keep. The survey said 45% to 55%, which is not a preference. Rather than dress that up, the report said there was no overwhelming preference and moved the decision to other grounds, with the verbatims explaining what each flow was actually good at: one let people scroll the status options first, which people found funnier, and the other was faster and produced more genuine pictures.

A workshop, not a presentation

I brought the synthesis to business stakeholders unfinished on purpose. Rather than presenting conclusions, I had them read it, contribute to it, and group the findings with me. We identified the key areas together, treated each area as a customer need, and generated and sharpened feature ideas against those areas, from a rough list down to the polished set of concepts.

The reason was practical. Stakeholders who grouped the findings themselves didn't have to be sold on the conclusions afterward, and the business ideas they arrived at were already validated against the research while we were in the room.

07

A second workstream: private events

Alongside the social research I worked on private events, which are events only invited people can see. Almost all of the design work turned out to be in the states around the event rather than the event page itself. Someone can arrive from a web browser or from the app, with the app installed or not, with access or without it, to an event that might be app-only or available on both. I sketched the whole matrix by hand before any of it went near Figma, because the number of combinations was the actual problem to solve.

08

Where it landed

The research set the direction rather than decorating a decision already made. The concepts that survived were the ones built on the venue relationship and on low-stakes, prompted participation. Direct messaging was deprioritized on the evidence that people already had iMessage and didn't want another inbox while out.

  • Rewards were treated as a comprehension problem first. Before adding more of them, the finding stood that users couldn't explain the ones they already had.
  • Friend-adding moved from a sign-up step to an ongoing one, because the research showed the graph is built in the first five minutes or never.
  • Concepts that would have spotlighted individual posts were flagged as a risk to the casual posting the feed runs on, which is not the conclusion an engagement target points you toward on its own.
  • Market context became a standing section of the reporting, which changed how the business thought about rolling the feature out campus by campus.
  • The pre-launch rounds shaped what shipped. The two post-launch rounds checked whether real behavior matched what the research predicted, and fed the next set of concepts.
