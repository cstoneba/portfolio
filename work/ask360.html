Ask360: AI Support Assistant

Requirements, conversation design, and research for the AI assistant built into Fiserv's client support portal, turning a search-then-retype support journey into a single conversation.

01

Context

Fiserv's client support portal is where financial-institution clients go when something isn't working. Getting to an answer was the problem. Knowledge articles and product documentation were hard to surface through search, and what did surface wasn't always current. When search failed, the client opened an inquiry and retyped the whole situation from scratch.

Ask360 is built to close that gap and make self-service actually work. A client describes the problem in their own words, gets documentation back for the products they actually license, and if that doesn't resolve it, opens an inquiry from inside the same conversation with the context already attached. One journey instead of three.

02

My role

I write the product requirements and user stories that translate what the assistant needs to do into something engineering, design, and the knowledge-platform team can actually build against and test. That includes the boundaries around what data the assistant is allowed to touch, not just what it should say.

03

Process

Making the answer specific to the client

A generic answer pulled from generic documentation is exactly what the old search already did badly. So the assistant needed to know which products a client actually licenses, alongside their account and case data, and answer against that instead of describing features they don't own. Defining which of those fields it could surface, and how much of each, shaped the requirements before any conversation design started.

Respecting what each user is allowed to do

Permissions vary between users inside the same client organization. Some can create inquiries, some can view projects or invoices, some can't. The assistant reads those permissions and adapts to them: it won't offer to open an inquiry for someone who has no rights to create one, and it won't reference an invoice that person can't see. Writing that as acceptance criteria meant enumerating the permission combinations and defining what the assistant should say in each.

Mapping the conversation

I partnered with engineering, design, and the knowledge-platform team to document 40+ conversational use cases for the portal, and, for each one, the edge cases: what happens when the assistant doesn't have enough data to answer, when a client asks something out of scope, and when the documentation can't resolve it and the client needs to open an inquiry instead. I wrote the acceptance criteria for each so engineering had a clear definition of "done" and QA had something concrete to test against.

Designing for whatever screen it opens on

Clients open the portal from a desk and from a phone between meetings, so the assistant is adaptive rather than desktop-only. The same conversation, documentation, and inquiry flow had to hold up at desktop, tablet, and mobile widths, which meant specifying how the chat panel, the returned articles, and the inquiry form reflow instead of assuming full-width layout.

Testing it against real clients

I led B2B user research directly with financial-institution clients, running one-on-one interviews plus a recurring monthly user group, to check whether the assistant's answers, and the handoff into creating an inquiry, actually matched how clients described problems in practice, not just how we assumed they would.

04

Collaboration & stakeholders

This only works if the same set of requirements makes sense to three different audiences at once: engineering needs to know exactly what's buildable and where the data boundaries are, the knowledge-platform team needs to know what content gaps to fill so the assistant has something accurate to draw from, and design needs the constraints on what the assistant can safely say before it designs the conversation itself. Keeping requirements consistent across those three groups, and re-aligning them every time research surfaced a new edge case, was most of the day-to-day work.
