Ask360: AI Support Assistant

Requirements, conversation design, and research for the AI assistant built into Fiserv's client support portal, turning a search-then-retype support journey into a single conversation.

01

Context

Fiserv's client support portal is where financial-institution clients go when something isn't working. Getting to an answer was the problem. Knowledge articles and product documentation were hard to surface through search, and what did surface wasn't always current. When search failed, the client opened an inquiry and retyped the whole situation from scratch.

Ask360 is built to close that gap and make self-service actually work. A client describes the problem in their own words, gets documentation back for the products they actually license, and if that doesn't resolve it, opens an inquiry from inside the same conversation with the context already attached. One journey instead of three.

02

My role

I own both the requirements and the design on this one. I write the user stories and acceptance criteria that engineering and the knowledge-platform team build against, including the boundaries around what data the assistant is allowed to touch. I also did the visual design and built the working prototype, using Figma Make and GitHub Copilot.

03

Process

Making the answer specific to the client

A generic answer pulled from generic documentation is exactly what the old search already did badly. So the assistant needed to know which products a client actually licenses, alongside their account and case data, and answer against that instead of describing features they don't own. Defining which of those fields it could surface, and how much of each, shaped the requirements before any conversation design started.

Respecting what each user is allowed to do

Permissions vary between users inside the same client organization. Some can create inquiries, some can view projects or invoices, some can't. The assistant reads those permissions and adapts to them: it won't offer to open an inquiry for someone who has no rights to create one, and it won't reference an invoice that person can't see. Writing that as acceptance criteria meant enumerating the permission combinations and defining what the assistant should say in each.

Mapping the conversation

I worked with engineering and the knowledge-platform team to document 40+ conversational use cases for the portal, and, for each one, the edge cases: what happens when the assistant doesn't have enough data to answer, when a client asks something out of scope, and when the documentation can't resolve it and the client needs to open an inquiry instead. I wrote the acceptance criteria for each so engineering had a clear definition of "done" and QA had something concrete to test against.

Designing it, not just specifying it

I did the visual design for the assistant and built it into a working prototype with Figma Make and GitHub Copilot, instead of handing over static screens. Something clickable meant the flows could be judged as an actual experience: typing a real question, reading what came back, deciding whether the answer was enough or an inquiry was still needed.

Designing for whatever screen it opens on

Clients open the portal from a desk and from a phone between meetings, so the assistant is adaptive rather than desktop-only. The same conversation, documentation, and inquiry flow had to hold up at desktop, tablet, and mobile widths, which meant specifying how the chat panel, the returned articles, and the inquiry form reflow instead of assuming full-width layout.

Testing it before it existed

I ran Wizard of Oz sessions with 10 client users, playing the assistant myself behind the prototype so people could work through real problems before any of the model behaviour was built. Where someone got stuck, described a problem in words the flows didn't anticipate, or expected an action the assistant never offered, I changed the flows and the copy between sessions. A recurring monthly user group kept that feedback coming after the sessions ended.

04

Collaboration & stakeholders

This only works if the same set of requirements makes sense to several audiences at once: engineering needs to know exactly what's buildable and where the data boundaries are, the knowledge-platform team needs to know which content gaps to fill so the assistant has something accurate to draw from, and the teams that own account and permissions data need to agree on precisely what can be exposed and to whom. Keeping requirements consistent across those groups, and re-aligning them every time testing surfaced a new edge case, was most of the day-to-day work.

05

Outcome

40+ conversational use cases documented with acceptance criteria
10 client users in Wizard of Oz testing
15% reduction in mean time to resolution and average handle time

Findings from the B2B research fed directly back into the requirements, and the changes they drove helped cut mean time to resolution and average handle time by 15% across the accounts we studied.
